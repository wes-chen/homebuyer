# Lesson 2: The down payment and PMI

The down payment is the chunk of the price you pay yourself — your starting
equity. Twenty percent is the traditional target, but it's a convention, not
a law, and it does three specific things for you.

First, **20% avoids PMI**. Second, a bigger down payment shrinks the loan and
the monthly payment. Third, in a competitive Bay Area bidding situation, a
large down payment makes your offer read as stronger and more certain to
close — sellers prefer buyers who look likely to clear appraisal and
underwriting.

What happens below 20%? Conventional loans allow as little as 3–5% down, but
the lender charges **private mortgage insurance (PMI)** — insurance that
protects the lender, not you, if you default. You pay the premium, typically
as a monthly add-on to your mortgage payment. PMI usually runs 0.3–1.5% of
the loan per year, priced on credit score and loan-to-value. A Bay Area
worked example: on a $1.5M home with 10% down ($150k), a $1.35M loan at ~0.5%
PMI adds roughly $560/month on top of PITI.

PMI is not permanent on conventional loans. You can request cancellation once
your loan-to-value hits 80% — through paying down the balance, the home
appreciating, or a new appraisal. At 78% LTV, federal law requires the
servicer to drop it automatically (track this yourself — servicers aren't
always prompt about the requestable 80%).

Where does the money come from? Savings, stock sales (common in the South
Bay), and family gifts — lenders require a signed gift letter and a paper
trail, no mattress cash. Raiding retirement accounts is generally a bad
trade: penalties and lost compounding, plus a 401(k) loan comes due if you
change jobs. Talk to a tax or financial professional before touching
retirement money for a house.

The Bay Area scale matters: 20% of $1.5M is $300k. That size is also where
loan categories shift — the 2026 conforming limit for Santa Clara and San
Mateo counties tops out at $1,249,125, so large loans cross into jumbo
territory, which lesson 3 covers.

Action: total up realistic down payment sources — savings, vested equity,
possible gifts. Don't count retirement. That number is your real starting
point.
