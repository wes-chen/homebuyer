# Lesson 8: Open houses: what to look at and ask

An open house is the only chance to test a listing against reality before you spend money on inspections or an offer. The listing showed you the best version of the house; the visit shows you the house itself. Go with a plan, not just a feeling.

**Walk it like a skeptic, in order.** Start outside: a sagging roofline, cracked stucco, a driveway that slopes toward the house, gutters that don't drain. In the Bay Area, water is the enemy — look for ceiling stains, bubbling paint, musty smells, and soft spots around windows and under sinks. Run every faucet, flush every toilet, flip the light switches. Open and close the windows — original single-pane windows in a 1960s tract home mean noise and drafts. Feel the floors for slopes or springiness, which can signal foundation movement (common near the Hayward fault and on hillside lots).

**The three questions that matter.** Ask the listing agent: (1) How long has the seller owned it, and why are they selling? (2) What's the offer date, and how many offers do you expect? (3) Are disclosures available to take home? In the Bay Area, offers are typically due within about a week of listing, so the open house is often your only window to decide whether to request the disclosure packet and line up pre-approval. Also ask about the sewer lateral — many South Bay cities require a sewer lateral inspection or replacement at sale, and that's a five-figure surprise if you miss it.

**Red flags that should slow you down.** Fresh paint on only one wall or ceiling (covering water damage), new flooring in just one room, a strong air freshener masking a smell, no access to the attic or crawl space, and a "recently remodeled" kitchen whose permits you can't verify. None of these is a deal-killer alone — that's what inspections are for (lesson 11) — but a cluster of them means budgeting extra for diligence.

**Watch the crowd, not just the house.** Count the sign-in sheet and the foot traffic. A packed open house in Palo Alto or Los Altos tells you the bidding will be competitive; a quiet one tells you the price may be soft. Listen to other visitors' questions, too — experienced buyers ask about the roof age, the foundation, and the permit history.

**Action:** this weekend, attend two open houses — one in your target price range and one above it. Bring this checklist: water signs, window and floor feel, roof age, sewer lateral, offer date. Write down one thing each house hid in its photos. You're building the instinct that turns a listing into a yes or a no in twenty minutes.
